Overview

Listening in on the lungs

A clinician listening through a stethoscope is trained to pick out crackles, wheezes, and other adventitious sounds that point toward conditions like COPD, pneumonia, or bronchiolitis. It's a skill that takes years to build and still varies from one listener to the next.

This project asks a narrower question: can a model learn that same signal directly from the raw audio? It's trained and evaluated on the ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database, a public benchmark built from real patient recordings and annotated breathing cycles, and used throughout as the standard test bed for this kind of classification work.

The data

6,898 breathing cycles, badly imbalanced

Each recording's annotation file marks the start and end of every breathing cycle, flagged for the presence of crackles or wheezes. Merging those cycles with each patient's diagnosis gives one label per breathing-cycle segment across eight conditions.

The distribution is heavily skewed toward COPD, which makes up roughly five out of every six segments in the dataset — a class imbalance that shapes everything downstream, from augmentation strategy to which per-class scores are trustworthy later on.

The pipeline

From waveform to diagnosis

Six stages turn a raw .wav recording into a predicted diagnosis, moving from audio loading through augmentation, feature extraction, and a convolutional classifier.

01

Load and trim

Every clip is loaded with librosa and trimmed to a fixed 2.5-second window (0.6s offset), so every input to the model has the same shape regardless of the original recording length.

02

Augment

Each clip is expanded into three versions — the original, a noise-injected copy, and a time-stretched and pitch-shifted copy — to give the model more variation to learn from and push back against the class imbalance.

03

Extract features

Four descriptors are computed per clip and averaged into a single feature vector: zero-crossing rate, chroma STFT, MFCCs, and RMS energy.

ZCR · CHROMA STFT · MFCC · RMS ENERGY
04

Scale and split

Features are standardized with a StandardScaler, then split 75/25 into training and test sets.

05

Train a 1D CNN

Four Conv1D + max-pooling blocks (256 → 256 → 128 → 64 filters) feed a dense layer with dropout and a softmax head over the eight diagnosis classes.

ADAM · CATEGORICAL CROSS-ENTROPY · 50 EPOCHS · BATCH 64 · REDUCE-LR-ON-PLATEAU
06

Evaluate

The trained model is scored on the held-out test set, with precision, recall, and F1 reported per class to see where it holds up and where it doesn't.

Reference

What the model is listening for

Crackles and wheezes are the two adventitious sounds the ICBHI annotations flag on every breathing cycle, and they're a big part of what separates the eight diagnosis classes. These are illustrative patterns to build intuition — not real patient recordings, and not your own audio from the panel above.

Normal breath

smooth, low-frequency, no clicks

A steady rise and fall in amplitude with no sharp transients — the envelope of ordinary inhale/exhale.

Wheeze

sustained, high-pitched, whistle-like

A tight, near-continuous high-frequency oscillation — shows up as a bright, narrow band on a spectrogram.

Crackle

brief, sharp clicks during inhale

Short, irregular spikes on top of an otherwise quiet baseline — brief pops rather than sustained tones.

Illustrative waveforms drawn for teaching purposes, not derived from patient data.

SUMMARY

High headline accuracy on this benchmark comes largely from the dominant COPD class. Scores on rare conditions (Asthma, LRTI) are built on a handful of test examples and shouldn't be read as reliable performance claims. This model has not been evaluated on external data, on consumer hardware, or against clinician-level baselines, and was never intended to inform real diagnostic decisions.

DATASET

ICBHI 2017 Respiratory Sound Database — patient recordings and breathing-cycle annotations released for the ICBHI 2017 Challenge, covering eight diagnosis labels.
